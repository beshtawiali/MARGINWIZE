How to Calculate Stacked Discounts (Without Overpaying)

By MarginWize Editorial Team • August 2026 • 5 Min Read

You've got a 25% off coupon and a store just texted you an extra 10% off code. Add them together and you'd expect 35% off, right? That's the assumption almost everyone makes — and it's wrong. Understanding how stacked discounts actually work isn't just a curiosity for shoppers; for retailers and e-commerce sellers, pricing this incorrectly can quietly erode margins on every multi-coupon sale you process.

Why Stacked Discounts Don't Simply Add Up

Here's the part that trips almost everyone up: when you apply a second discount, it doesn't apply to the original price. It applies to the price that's already been reduced by the first discount. Each discount compounds on top of the previous one instead of stacking side by side.

The Real Compounding Formula:
Final Price = Original Price × (1 − Discount 1) × (1 − Discount 2)
Compare that to what most people assume:
Wrong Assumption: Final Price = Original Price × (1 − Discount 1 − Discount 2)

These two formulas produce different answers, and the gap gets bigger the larger your discounts are.

A Real Example With Real Numbers

Let's say you're buying something priced at $200, with a 25% coupon and an additional 10% stacked coupon.

  • ×
    What most people expect (adding percentages):
    25% + 10% = 35% off → $200 × (1 − 0.35) = $130
  • ✓
    What actually happens (compounding):
    Step 1: $200 × (1 − 0.25) = $150 (after the first 25% discount)
    Step 2: $150 × (1 − 0.10) = $135 (after the second 10% discount, applied to the already-reduced $150, not the original $200)

The real final price is $135 — not $130. That's a $5 difference on a single $200 item. On a $2,000 order, that same misunderstanding is worth $50. The effective combined discount here isn't 35% at all; it's 32.5%. Combined discounts are always slightly less generous than simple addition suggests, because the second discount is working with a smaller base.

Why This Matters More Than People Realize

This isn't just a math curiosity — it shows up in real financial situations constantly:

  • For online shoppers stacking a coupon code with a site-wide sale: if you're trying to hit a specific budget, adding percentages will make you think you're paying less than you actually will at checkout.
  • For retailers running promotional stacking (like "extra 10% off already reduced items"): if you price your promotions assuming discounts add linearly, you'll give away more margin than your promotional plan intended. A "25% + 10% = 35% off" promotion actually only costs you 32.5% of revenue on that item, not 35% — the difference matters at scale across thousands of transactions.
  • For anyone using cashback plus a coupon plus a loyalty discount: three-way stacking is increasingly common with retailer apps and browser extensions. The compounding effect gets even more pronounced with each additional discount layer, and manual mental math becomes unreliable fast.

Does Stacking Order Matter?

Here's a detail that surprises a lot of people: when you're applying pure percentage discounts, the order you apply them in doesn't change the final price. $200 discounted 25% then 10% lands at the same $135 as discounting 10% then 25% first. Multiplication is commutative — the math works out the same regardless of sequence.

Where order does matter is when you're mixing a percentage discount with a flat dollar-amount coupon (like "$20 off"). Applying the flat discount before or after the percentage discount produces different results, because a percentage applied to a smaller base removes fewer dollars. If you have the choice, apply the percentage discount first and the flat-dollar coupon second — it typically works out slightly better for the buyer.

What About Sales Tax on Top of Discounts?

One more layer that catches people off guard: sales tax is calculated on the final discounted price, not the original price. If your $200 item drops to $135 after stacked discounts and your local sales tax is 8%, tax is calculated on $135, not $200. That's $10.80 in tax rather than $16 — another place where doing the math in the wrong order leads to the wrong number showing up at checkout.

The Bottom Line

Stacked discounts always produce a smaller total reduction than simply adding the percentages together, and the gap grows as your discounts get larger. For a single small purchase, the difference might only be a few dollars. Across a shopping cart, a bulk order, or a retail promotion running at scale, it adds up to real money — money that either stays in your pocket as a smart shopper, or stays in your margin as a business owner who priced the promotion correctly from the start.
