The Complete Markup Formula Guide: How Retailers Price for Profit

By MarginWize Editorial Team • August 2026 • 6 Min Read

Every retail price tag hides a decision. Behind that $49.99 sweater or that $12 sandwich is a markup calculation someone made — sometimes carefully, sometimes by guesswork. Get that calculation wrong consistently, and even a business with strong sales can quietly bleed profit month after month without anyone noticing until the year-end numbers come in short.

This guide breaks down exactly how to calculate markup, walks through the cost-plus pricing method retailers actually use, and covers the industry benchmarks that tell you whether your pricing is competitive or leaving money on the table.

What Markup Actually Means

Markup is the amount added to a product's cost to determine its selling price, expressed as a percentage of that cost. If you buy a product for $40 and sell it for $60, you've added $20 in markup — a 50% markup on cost.

The Markup Percentage Formula:
Markup (%) = ((Selling Price − Cost) / Cost) × 100
Rearranged to find your selling price directly:
Selling Price = Cost × (1 + Markup %)

So a $40 product with a target 50% markup becomes: $40 × 1.50 = $60.

The Cost-Plus Pricing Method, Step by Step

Cost-plus pricing is the most widely used pricing method in retail because it's simple and it guarantees you never sell below your cost. Here's how it's actually applied in practice:

  • Step 1: Calculate your true cost. This isn't just what you paid the supplier — it should include shipping, import duties, packaging, and any direct handling costs. A product that costs $35 from the supplier but $5 more to ship and package has a true cost of $40, not $35.
  • Step 2: Decide your target markup percentage. This varies enormously by industry (more on that below), and should account for your operating expenses — rent, staff, marketing — not just leave you with a number that looks good on paper.
  • Step 3: Apply the formula. Multiply cost by (1 + markup percentage) to get your selling price.
  • Step 4: Check it against the market. A mathematically correct markup that prices you 40% above every competitor selling the identical product won't move inventory, regardless of how clean the formula is.

Average Retail Markup by Industry: What's Normal?

Markup benchmarks vary dramatically by category, and knowing where your industry typically sits helps you sanity-check your own pricing:

  • Clothing and apparel: Often marked up 100–250%, sometimes higher for designer or boutique brands, because inventory turnover is slower and seasonal markdowns eat into eventual realized margin.
  • Restaurants (food cost): Typically target a 200–300% markup on raw ingredient cost, since labor, rent, and overhead consume a large share of revenue that isn't visible in the ingredient cost alone.
  • Electronics and appliances: Often run much thinner, sometimes 10–30% markup, because of intense price comparison shopping and thin margins on established brands.
  • Furniture: Commonly marked up 100–200%, reflecting slower turnover, higher storage and shipping costs, and lower purchase frequency.
  • Jewelry: Can run 100–300%+ depending on the segment, since perceived value plays a much larger role than in commodity retail categories.

If your markup sits far outside your category's typical range without a clear reason (premium positioning, unique sourcing, exceptional service), it's worth investigating whether you're pricing yourself out of the market or leaving profit on the table.

The Mistake That Quietly Costs Retailers the Most

Here's the trap that catches even experienced sellers: applying the same flat markup percentage across an entire product line, regardless of how each item actually sells.

Say you apply a uniform 50% markup to both a fast-moving $20 item and a slow-moving $200 item. The dollar profit on the $200 item ($100) looks great on paper — but if it sits in inventory for eight months before selling while the $20 item turns over weekly, the fast-moving item is actually generating far more profit per dollar of inventory tied up over the same period. Markup percentage alone doesn't account for how quickly that markup translates into actual cash.

This is why experienced retailers often think in terms of both markup and inventory turnover together, not markup in isolation. A lower markup on a fast-moving item can outperform a high markup on something that barely moves.

Markup Isn't the Same as Margin — And Mixing Them Up Is Expensive

If you take one thing from this guide beyond the formula itself, it's this: markup and profit margin are calculated differently, and confusing the two when setting prices is one of the most common — and costly — mistakes in small business pricing. A 50% markup does not produce a 50% margin; it actually produces a 33.3% margin, because markup is calculated on cost while margin is calculated on the final selling price.

Setting Markup for International or Multi-Currency Pricing

If you're selling across the US, UK, and Canada, your target markup percentage often needs to flex slightly by market — not because the formula changes, but because landed costs do. A product shipped to the UK may carry VAT considerations and different import duties than the same product sold domestically in the US, while Canadian sellers often face different customs thresholds entirely. The core markup formula stays identical; what changes is the true cost figure you're plugging into it, once all region-specific costs are accounted for.

Bottom Line

The markup formula itself takes thirty seconds to calculate once you know your true cost and target percentage. The harder part — and the part that actually determines whether a business is profitable — is choosing the right target percentage for your specific product, market, and turnover rate, and staying disciplined about including every real cost before you apply that formula.
