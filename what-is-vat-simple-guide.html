What Is VAT? A Simple Guide to Value-Added Tax

By MarginWize Editorial Team • August 2026 • 5 Min Read

If you've ever seen a price in London and wondered why the receipt total didn't match the shelf tag, or tried to run an online store that ships to both the US and Europe and got confused by two completely different tax systems, you've bumped into VAT. It's one of the most widely used tax systems in the world, and yet it remains genuinely confusing to people who grew up with a different model.

What VAT Actually Is

VAT stands for Value-Added Tax. It's a consumption tax applied at each stage of production and distribution, not just at the final sale. Every business in the supply chain — the raw material supplier, the manufacturer, the wholesaler, the retailer — charges VAT on what they sell and can typically reclaim the VAT they paid on their own purchases. The end consumer is the only one who can't reclaim it, which is why VAT ultimately functions as a tax on final consumption, even though it's collected in pieces along the way.

Over 170 countries use some form of VAT, including the entire European Union, the UK, and most of Asia and Africa. Standard rates vary widely — the UK sits at 20%, most EU countries fall between 19% and 25%, and some countries apply reduced rates to essentials like food or children's clothing.

VAT vs Sales Tax: The Difference That Trips Up US Businesses

This is where most confusion comes from, especially for US-based sellers expanding internationally. Sales tax, the system used across most of the United States, is only charged once — at the final point of sale to the consumer. Businesses further up the supply chain don't charge or collect it on business-to-business transactions.

VAT works differently. It's collected at every stage, and businesses net out what they've collected against what they've paid, remitting only the difference to the government. For a business, this means VAT involves more bookkeeping — tracking input VAT (what you paid) and output VAT (what you charged) — but it also means the tax burden is spread more evenly across the supply chain rather than landing entirely at checkout.

For a shopper, the practical difference is usually invisible. Prices in VAT countries are almost always displayed inclusive of tax — what you see on the shelf is what you pay. In the US, sales tax is added at checkout, which is why the total is always higher than the sticker price.

How to Calculate VAT: The Two Directions

There are two calculations you'll run into, depending on what you're starting with.

Adding VAT to a net price (going from net to gross):

Gross Price Formula:
Gross Price = Net Price × (1 + VAT Rate)

Example: A $100 net price with 20% VAT becomes $100 × 1.20 = $120 gross.

Extracting VAT from a gross price (going from gross to net):

Net Price Formula:
Net Price = Gross Price / (1 + VAT Rate)
VAT Amount = Gross Price − Net Price

Example: If a receipt shows $120 total with 20% VAT already included, the net price is $120 / 1.20 = $100, meaning $20 of that total was VAT.

This second calculation is the one people get wrong most often. A common mistake is taking 20% of the gross price directly ($120 × 20% = $24) instead of first dividing out the tax-inclusive base. That $4 error might look small, but it compounds quickly across large invoices or bulk transactions, and it's the exact kind of mistake that causes bookkeeping to not reconcile at month-end.

VAT Refunds: What Travelers Actually Get Back

If you've shopped while traveling abroad, you may have seen "VAT refund" signage at stores or airports. Many countries allow non-resident visitors to reclaim VAT paid on goods they're taking home with them, since the tax is meant to apply to domestic consumption, not exports.

The process typically requires the purchase to exceed a minimum threshold, proper documentation at the point of sale, and validation at customs before departure. The refund isn't always the full VAT amount either — most refund services charge a processing fee, so travelers often receive somewhere between 10-15% of the purchase price back, even on goods taxed at 20%.

Why This Matters for Your Pricing

If you're running a business that sells internationally, understanding which system applies — and where — isn't optional. Charging VAT incorrectly, or failing to register for VAT once you cross a country's sales threshold, can result in penalties that are far more expensive than the tax itself would have been. Many countries also require VAT registration once your sales into that market cross a specific revenue threshold, even if your business is based somewhere else entirely.

For day-to-day pricing decisions, the calculation itself is simple once you know which direction you're working in. The harder part is usually knowing the correct rate for the specific country, product category, and whether any reduced-rate exemptions apply.
